Key Demographic Indicators and Rates

Population growth rate, 1990–present Calculated from Eurostat population stocks. Standard annual growth rate = 100 × ((Population_t / Population_t-1) − 1). Greece uses demo_pjan; NUTS-2 and NUTS-3 use demo_r_gind3 / January population trajectories. Why it matters: population growth rate shows the direction and speed of demographic change from one year to the next. It helps users distinguish slow decline, rapid contraction, recovery, or sudden revisions that may need cautious interpretation.

Live vs calculated: Estimated counts and Estimated % distribution are calculated from live Eurostat regional age structure plus stored municipality baseline totals. Exponential rate r is calculated as ln(Population_t / Population_t-1). The chart displays 100 × r so it stays on a percentage-like scale. For small annual changes, annual % change and 100 × r will usually be very close.
Sharp discontinuities should be interpreted cautiously. The clearest likely census-linked revision effect is around the 2022 revision window; earlier movements more often reflect underlying demographic change rather than a break in series.

Population index (1990 = 100) Calculated from Eurostat population stocks. Index = 100 × (Population_t / Population_1990), so 1990 equals 100 for each geography. Greece uses demo_pjan; NUTS-2 and NUTS-3 use demo_r_gind3 / January population trajectories. Why it matters: the index converts different starting population sizes onto the same scale, making it easier to compare long-run trajectories across places. Values above 100 mean the population is larger than in 1990; values below 100 mean it is smaller.

Shows the indexed population trajectory for the currently selected profile geography.

Age-Structure Indicators All indicators below are derived from the latest available male and female age-group counts for the selected geography. Shares are percentages of the total population; ratios compare older and younger age groups.

Population pyramid (latest) Source: Eurostat population by age group and sex. Bars show each age group as a share of the male or female population in the selected geography. Younger ages appear at the bottom and older ages toward the top. Why it matters: the pyramid makes it easy to see whether the population is youthful, ageing, balanced, or shaped by migration and cohort shocks. It is one of the quickest visual summaries of demographic structure.

Shares are percentages within each sex. Younger ages are at the bottom.

Age-group shares (male vs female) Source: Eurostat population by age group and sex. The line chart shows each age group as a percentage of the male and female populations separately, using the latest available year for the selected geography. Why it matters: the line chart makes sex differences easier to compare across age groups than the pyramid alone. It is especially useful for seeing where women outnumber men at older ages or where cohorts differ by sex.

Compares age-group shares within the male and female populations separately for the currently selected geography.

Estimated municipality age profile How it was created: this version does not use directly observed municipality-by-age data. Instead, it takes the selected region's overall age structure and applies that pattern to the selected municipality's total population. That produces an estimate of how many people may fall into each age group. How to interpret it: in count view, taller bars usually mean larger estimated numbers of people in those age groups. In percentage view, you are looking at the municipality's estimated age composition. Because all municipalities in the selected region currently inherit the same regional age pattern, the percentage shape will look very similar across municipalities in this version. Source: this chart combines two inputs. Municipality totals come from 2021 Greek census municipality populations stored in the dashboard blueprint. Age-pattern data come from live Eurostat regional age structure (demo_pjan and demo_r_pjangrp3). The municipality age bars are then estimated inside the dashboard.

Abridged life tablesIn simple terms, a life table is a model population that shows how many people would still be alive at each age if they experienced the current age-specific death rates throughout life. It is commonly used to summarize mortality, estimate life expectancy, and answer survival questions.

In plain language: a life table is a simple model of survival across the life course. It lets you see how mortality builds up with age, what life expectancy looks like, and the chance of surviving from one age to another.

Projection type help

These scenarios come from Eurostat's regional EUROPOP2019 projection framework for proj_19rp3.

Baseline projections are Eurostat's reference regional scenario. This is the main projection path used for comparison.

Sensitivity test: No migration sets migration to zero for both international and internal components in every projection year. Population change then comes only from births and deaths.

Sensitivity test: No interregional migration sets internal migration between regions to zero, while international migration remains in the projection.

These are not deterministic forecasts. They are scenario outcomes under stated assumptions.
